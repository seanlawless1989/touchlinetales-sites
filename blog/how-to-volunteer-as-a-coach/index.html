To volunteer as a coach with a grassroots youth team, find a role through your local club or county FA, complete a DBS check (free for volunteers), take an entry-level badge like the FA's Introduction to Coaching Football, and run one training session and one match a week. This guide walks through each step, then shows what season one actually looks like.

What does a volunteer coach actually do?

You coach for an hour a week. Everything else is admin, and the admin is the job.

The hour of training is the easy part

Planning a session for Under 8s takes maybe 20 minutes once you've done it a few times. The Introduction to Coaching Football course from England Football Learning gives you the basics: safeguarding awareness, session design, and how to run a warm-up that isn't chaos. It's online, entry-level, and aimed at exactly your situation: a parent stepping up because nobody else would.

The hidden admin: availability, line-ups, results

The real workload is Sunday night. Chasing availability in the group chat, retyping the line-up, posting the result, and picking a player of the match. That's two to three hours a week on top of training, and it never appears on any badge. Tools like our own Touchline Tales exist to shrink exactly this: sharing results with parents in one place instead of twenty WhatsApp messages.

How do you find volunteer coach jobs near you?

Start with your own child's club

The easiest route is the team your child already plays for. Most grassroots clubs are short of coaches every single season, and the manager of your child's Under 7s will likely say yes before you finish asking. Turn up to training, offer to run a warm-up, and mention you're happy to take a badge. That's usually all it takes.

County FAs, club websites and local leagues

If your child's club is full, your County FA lists clubs looking for volunteers, and local league websites often post requests too. Club sites sometimes have a "volunteers needed" page. Ask the club secretary or welfare officer directly; they hold the DBS process and will tell you which age groups need help. Our welfare officer checklist walks through what they'll need from you: https://touchlinetales.club/guides/welfare-officer-checklist/

What to ask before you say yes

Which age group, how many kids, and is there a second coach or are you alone? Ask about training times, whether the club pays for your badge, and who handles pitch bookings. A club that answers those clearly is one worth joining.

What checks and qualifications do you need?

Two things gate your first session: a DBS check and a safeguarding qualification. Badges come later. Clubs will not put you on the touchline until the first two are done, so start them the week you volunteer.

The DBS check, step by step

Youth football volunteers need an Enhanced DBS check with a check of the barred lists. You cannot apply for it yourself for club work: the FA processes it through its own online portal, verified by your club's welfare officer. The check is free for volunteers (you pay nothing; paid coaches do), and FA checks are valid for three years before needing renewal. The government's overview of the process is here: DBS checks explained. Expect two to four weeks, so apply before pre-season, not before your first match.

Safeguarding training and the welfare officer's role

The welfare officer books your DBS, points you at the right courses, and is your first call if a parent or player raises a concern. Every FA youth club must have one. If your club stores children's data, including photos, results and dates of birth, it should have a clear policy; our own is at https://touchlinetales.club/privacy/.

How much does it cost and who pays?

Most volunteer coaches pay nothing for their FA courses and DBS check. Clubs and leagues typically cover both, because they need qualified coaches to keep teams running. Ask the club secretary or welfare officer before you book anything yourself, and confirm in writing who is paying.

What clubs typically cover

The DBS check, entry-level badges and safeguarding course are the usual club-funded items. Some clubs also pay for first-aid training. If your club won't cover a course, your county FA sometimes offers funding for volunteer coaches, so it's worth asking before paying out of your own pocket.

Expenses and kit norms

Coaching kit varies. Many clubs supply a jacket or polo; others expect you to wear your own. Cones, bibs and balls normally come from the club's kit bag, though plenty of coaches buy their own whistle and end up out of pocket for extras. Keep receipts and ask whether the club reimburses them, since small costs add up across a season. How do you prepare your first session?

Keep it simple: one theme, lots of ball contact

Pick one theme, such as stopping with the ball or passing to a teammate, and build the whole hour around it. Every child should touch the ball as often as possible: a ball each where you can, small groups, short queues. If more than a minute passes while you explain, they have stopped listening. Arrive ten minutes early with cones out and bibs sorted so you can greet players instead of setting up.

Age-appropriate expectations from Under 6s to teens

Under 6s and Under 7s will chase a ball, fall over and wander off mid-drill; that is normal, not a discipline problem. By Under 11s you can run simple shapes and rotate positions, and teens will want to know why they are doing something before they buy into it. For rugby volunteers, the RFU sets out what entry-level coaching courses and DBS checks involve before you take a session: England Rugby's guidance on coaching courses and vetting.

A simple session template

A workable shape: five minutes of arrival games, ten of a fun warm-up, fifteen on your theme, fifteen on a small-sided game that uses it, and five for a match-like finish. Write it on a card in your pocket and delegate the warm-up to a parent helper. Once the season starts, you can log how each session went and what to revisit on your team site's changelog so week two builds on week one.

What does the first season actually look like?

Pre-season: registration and comms setup

Before the first session, you'll collect sign-ups, confirm ages against FA rules, and get every parent into one place. Set up your group chat, your fixture list, and a simple way to record who's available each week. An hour spent here saves a Sunday-night scramble every week after.

Matchday rhythm and fair playing time

A typical week is one training session, a fixture, and a message or two about availability. On the day you arrive early, set out bibs and goals, pick line-ups, and make sure every child gets sensible minutes. Rolling subs at Under 7s to Under 11s make rotation easier; at older ages you'll need a plan so fair playing time doesn't slip.

Recording the season: results, player of the match, first goals

Most first-time coaches keep nothing. By May nobody remembers who scored the first goal or won player of the match in October. A simple record, child by child, turns into something families genuinely treasure. That's the gap Touchline Tales was built for: every match and milestone kept in your club's colours, without you retyping anything. A shared spreadsheet works too, if that's what you'll actually maintain.

How do you keep parents informed without drowning in WhatsApp?

Most parent-coaches run the whole season through one group chat, and it works until it doesn't. Kick-off times get buried under forty "thumbs up" replies, the line-up from last week is three scrolls away, and new parents join mid-season with no way to catch up.

Why the group chat breaks down

WhatsApp has no memory structure. There is one thread per team, so fixture details, availability, and "who has the bibs?" all compete for the same space. Anything worth keeping, like player of the match awards or a first goal, disappears within days. The FA expects coaches working with youth teams to complete safeguarding training (FA safeguarding training requirements), and part of that duty is keeping communication clear and on record rather than lost in chat scroll.

How do you stay sane and keep volunteering?

Recruit an assistant and a team admin

You cannot run sessions, manage the group chat, and sort matchday admin alone. Ask two parents in the first fortnight: one to run warm-ups alongside you, one to collect subs, chase availability, and type the line-up. Most will say yes if you name the job and keep it small.

Split the load explicitly rather than hoping it happens. An assistant covers sessions when work drags you late; an admin covers the messages. If you also use a tool like our own Touchline Tales to hold fixtures and results in one place, fewer questions land in your inbox in the first place.

Lean on the club, not just yourself

Your club secretary and welfare officer have seen every first-season wobble before. Ask them about mentoring, shared session plans, or borrowing kit rather than buying your own. If the role is eating your evenings by February, say so early. Clubs would rather adjust than lose a coach who quietly burned out.

FAQ

Can I volunteer as a coach with no playing or coaching experience?

Yes. Most grassroots coaches start as parents who simply said yes when nobody else would. The entry-level badges, such as the FA's Introduction to Coaching Football, are designed for exactly that situation and assume no prior experience. Turn up to training, offer to run a warm-up, and take the online course; your club will guide you through the rest.

How long does a volunteer coach DBS check take?

Expect two to four weeks from application to clearance. Because the FA processes it through its own portal with your club's welfare officer verifying identity, you cannot apply yourself directly. The check is free for volunteers and valid for three years. Apply before pre-season rather than the week before your first match, or you will be stuck watching from the car park.

Do volunteer coaches get paid anything at all?

No, the role itself is unpaid, but you should rarely be out of pocket. Clubs and county FAs typically cover the DBS check, entry-level badges, and safeguarding training, and some fund first-aid courses too. Confirm in writing who is paying before you book anything. Keep receipts for extras like whistles and ask whether the club reimburses them.

How many hours a week does a volunteer coach spend on the team?

Roughly three to four hours a week in total. The training session itself is about an hour, planning takes twenty minutes once you have done it a few times, and the hidden workload is matchday admin: chasing availability, picking line-ups, and posting results, which adds two to three hours, mostly on Sunday nights.

What's the difference between a volunteer coach and an assistant coach?

The head coach runs the session, picks line-ups, and owns the decisions on matchday. An assistant supports: running warm-ups, managing subs, and covering sessions when the head coach is unavailable. If you are stepping up, recruit an assistant early and name the job clearly. Most parents will say yes to a small, defined role rather than a vague one.
