Key takeaways

  • A 60-minute training session football plan splits into a 10-minute warm-up, 20-minute technical block, 25-minute small-sided game, and 5-minute cool-down.
  • Pick one theme per session, drawn from what went wrong on Sunday, and run it through warm-up, drill, and game.

A good training session football plan for a grassroots team fits four blocks into one hour: a 10-minute warm-up, a 20-minute technical drill, a 25-minute small-sided game, and a 5-minute cool-down. This guide gives you that structure as a copy-and-fill template, so your Tuesday planning takes 15 minutes, not your whole evening.

What does a good 1 hour football training session look like?

Split the hour into four blocks: 10 minutes of warm-up, 20 minutes of technical work, 25 minutes of a small-sided game, and 5 minutes to cool down and talk. The FA's youth football guidance recommends age-appropriate formats and shorter session structures for younger age groups, so scale the blocks to your players (The FA).

The proportions matter more than the exact minutes. Roughly a third technical, half playing, keeps energy up and gives you a natural chance to coach in the game rather than lecture over it. Under 6s to Under 8s can carry less technical time, so shift a few minutes from drills to the game.

Keep the warm-up with a ball at every player's feet. Save one clear coaching point for the game block, and use the cool-down to name your player of the match and preview Sunday.

How do you plan a football training session in 15 minutes?

You do it with a fixed routine and one decision per week. Everything else stays the same: same arrival game, same shape of the hour, same finish. The only thing you change is the theme.

Pick one theme, not five

Choose a single focus for the session: passing, first touch, defending, or shooting. One theme runs through the warm-up, the drill, and the small-sided game, so players hear the same idea three ways instead of three ideas once.

To pick your theme in under a minute, ask two questions. What went wrong on Sunday? What did you promise the players you'd work on? That's your session.

Then fill in the blanks: arrival activity, one drill, one game, one finish. Write it on your phone in your notes app or on the back of a team sheet. If you want the session to reach parents too, the same notes can feed your matchday write-up (https://touchlinetales.club/guides/share-results-with-parents/).

Fifteen minutes, one theme, done by 9pm Tuesday.

Warm-up: 0-10 minutes

Every player with a ball from the first minute, not a line of them jogging laps. Tag games with a ball at their feet get heart rates up and touches in. UK Coaching's guidance on warming up stresses raising body temperature gradually and making the warm-up specific to what follows, so keep a ball involved (UK Coaching).

Technical block: 10-25 minutes

Pick one skill, nothing more. Demonstrate it once, get them working in pairs or threes, and change the drill every four minutes before attention drifts. Write the drill name in the blank so you remember what you ran.

Small-sided game: 25-50 minutes

The longest block, because this is where they actually learn. Play 4v4 or 5v5 and add one condition, such as "a goal only counts if everyone has touched the ball", to force the skill you just coached. Rotate teams so playing time stays fair.

Cool-down and team talk: 50-60 minutes

Two minutes of easy jogging and stretching, then a huddle: name the player of the match, one thing you liked, one focus for Sunday. How should the template change for Under 6s to Under 11s?

The 60-minute shape stays the same at every age; what changes is how long each block runs and how much explanation you give.

For Under 6s and Under 7s, shrink every block. Warm-up becomes five minutes of tag or races, the drill becomes a single game like sharks and islands, and the small-sided match takes half the session. FA youth formats at this age are 5v5 with rolling substitutions, so build the match around everyone getting time on the ball rather than positions. Give instructions in one sentence, then let them play.

At Under 8s to Under 10s the formats move to 7v7, so you can introduce two or three passes of instruction: a shape, a throw-in routine, a simple "first touch away from the press" cue. Keep the drill-to-match ratio at roughly 30 minutes of match play, because that is where they learn most. By Under 11s, playing 9v9, the same template works with a longer drill block and a five-minute chat at the end about player of the match and what you will work on next week.

One thing never changes: safeguarding. If anything about a child's welfare comes up mid-session, your club's welfare officer is the person to tell, and this welfare officer checklist (https://touchlinetales.club/guides/welfare-officer-checklist/) covers what to pass on and when.

What equipment do you need for a grassroots training session?

The template needs less than you think: one ball per player if you can get them, ten flat cones, six tall cones, two pop-up goals or corner-flag goals, ten bibs in two colours, and a whistle. That single bag covers the warm-up, every dribbling grid, and the small-sided games at the end.

Bibs are the one item coaches under-buy. With two clear colours you can split any drill in seconds, which matters when you have 45 minutes of actual coaching time.

Two checks before you set up. First, count your players against your helpers: FA safeguarding guidance sets coach-to-player ratios for youth sessions, and if you are short of adults, shrink the session rather than widen the supervision. Second, put the spare balls in a net bag so a stray shot never costs you two minutes of retrieval.

How do you keep every player involved in a mixed-ability session?

The trick is to design drills where nobody stands still long enough to switch off. Small-sided games (3v3 or 4v4) mean every player touches the ball every few seconds, instead of queuing behind eight others in a line.

For your stronger players, add a constraint rather than a lecture: they must use their weaker foot, or play one-touch. For your quieter players, give them a role that forces involvement, like starting every restart.

On fair playing time, decide your rota before Sunday night, not on the touchline. If you rotate pairs each quarter and write it down once, you stop the pattern where the same three kids always come off first.

One practical habit: after the session, note who got what. A team site in your club's colours keeps a per-player record across the season, so "did my child get a fair run-out?" is a question you can answer with evidence, not memory.

How do you remember what you coached and share it with parents?

Keep two records: what you planned, and what actually happened. After each session, spend two minutes noting the warm-up, the main drill, and one thing each player did well. By week ten you will know whether you have covered shooting, defending and first touch, or just repeated the same three drills since September.

For sharing, move the plan out of the group chat. Post the session outline once, in one message, rather than answering the same "what time is training?" question twenty times. Parents do not need the coaching detail; they need the day, the time and what to bring.

If your club wants a proper home for this, a team site works better than a chat thread. Tools like Spond handle scheduling and messages in one place, and a team site in your club's colours can keep every match, milestone and session note in one place all season.

Common mistakes when planning a football training session

The most common mistake is cramming too much in. Four new drills in 60 minutes means nobody masters anything; pick two themes and repeat them across weeks.

Second is too much standing around. Long queues for one drill lose Under 8s completely, so split the squad into small groups or use more balls than you think you need.

Third is planning for your best player. Set the drill so your weakest player can succeed and add a challenge layer for the strongest.

Fourth is skipping the warm-up because time is short. Five minutes with a ball counts, and it settles late arrivals.

Last is not writing anything down. Fill in the template on your phone before you leave the car park, or Sunday's plan starts from a blank page again.

FAQ

How long should a training session for football be for Under 7s?

Keep it to 45 minutes, or 60 at most with heavy shrinking of each block. Five minutes of tag, one game like sharks and islands, then half the session as a 5v5 match with rolling subs. Give instructions in one sentence, then let them play. Anything longer and attention goes before the whistle does.

Can I run a football training session with 8 players?

Yes, and the template works better with 8 than with 18. Run the warm-up as a dribbling grid, the technical block in two pairs, and finish with a 4v4 that fills the whole game block. Small numbers mean more touches per player, so you can coach the theme more often without anyone queuing.

How many coaches do you need for a youth training session?

Follow the FA's coach-to-player ratios for youth sessions, and count your helpers against your squad before you set up. If you are short of adults, shrink the session rather than widen the supervision. One lead coach plus one helper covers most grassroots groups; the helper manages queues and rotates teams so you can coach.

Should training be the same night every week?

Yes. A fixed night and time is what makes the 15-minute plan possible: same arrival game, same shape of the hour, same finish, one new theme each week. Parents build routines around it, so the "what time is training?" messages stop, and you stop replanning the logistics every Tuesday on top of the football.

What do I do if half the team doesn't turn up to training?

Run it anyway with a smaller version of the same plan. Drop to 3v3, shorten the technical block, and give the extra touches to whoever showed up. Never cancel at the last minute; the players there deserve a session. Note who attended, then set your Sunday playing-time rota with that attendance in mind.
