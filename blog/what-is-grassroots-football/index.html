Grassroots football in plain terms: the definition

Grassroots football is the amateur game played by volunteers: local clubs and leagues where coaches, referees and committee members are mostly parents giving up evenings and weekends, unpaid. It covers everything below the professional pyramid, from Under 6s kicking a ball about on a council pitch to open-age Sunday league sides.

The FA defines it as the largest part of the game in England, with hundreds of thousands of volunteers making weekly fixtures possible (The FA). If you run one team, book a pitch, pick a line-up and post the result in the group chat, you are grassroots football.

Clubs are typically small charities or community groups, funded by player subscriptions, local sponsors and grants, and affiliated to a county FA. That structure matters later, because it decides who signs off kit, fixtures and welfare rules for your team.

What does grassroots football mean compared to academy and semi-pro football?

Grassroots football is amateur football played at local clubs, run by volunteers, where nobody is paid to play. Academies sit above that: players are scouted, train several times a week and are on a professional pathway with a club. Semi-pro sits between the two, where players at Step 1 to Step 5 of the non-league pyramid earn some money but the club still isn't fully professional.

If your Under 8s side is parent-coached and plays on a Sunday, that's grassroots. The rest of this guide assumes you're there, and if you're already sharing results with parents each week, you're doing the core job.

How is a grassroots football club structured?

Most grassroots clubs start as one team with a manager, a coach and a WhatsApp group. The structure only gets more formal when more teams join.

The committee roles that keep a club running

A multi-team club is run by a volunteer committee: a chair who chairs meetings and signs off decisions, a secretary who deals with league correspondence and player registrations, a treasurer who collects subs and pays for pitch hire and kit, and a welfare officer who handles safeguarding checks and is the named contact for the county FA. Each team still has its own manager and coaches, but the committee owns the club's affiliation, its bank account and its discipline record. If you are starting a second team, you inherit these roles rather than inventing them, which is why new teams usually grow out of existing clubs instead of launching alone. The practical consequence for a parent-coach is that you rarely run the club, you run a team inside one, and the paperwork that is not matchday admin belongs to someone else on the committee.

Leagues and county FAs: who organises what

Your league schedules fixtures and handles results; your county FA handles discipline, affiliations and welfare. Playing formats, like pitch size and match length, are set nationally by age group, from Under 7s to Under 11s. Your club's job is to keep a season's record worth showing parents, which is what we built Touchline Tales to do.

What is FA grassroots youth football and which rules matter by age group?

The FA sets youth football rules by age group, and they change more than most volunteer coaches expect between Under 7s and Under 11s. The biggest shifts: 5v5 at Under 7s and Under 8s, 7v7 from Under 9s, then 9v9 at Under 11s and 12s before 11v11 at Open age. Pitch size, goal size and ball size all step up alongside the format.

Format changes by age: pitch size, ball size, playing time

Under 7s and 8s play with a size 3 ball on a small pitch with no offside. Under 9s and 10s move to 7v7, a bigger pitch and retreat lines for goal kicks. Under 11s step up again to 9v9 with a size 4 ball and offside introduced. Match lengths stay short across the board, and fair playing time matters more than the scoreline.

The welfare officer role applies at every age group, and our welfare officer checklist covers the essentials.

Who funds grassroots football? Sponsors, grants and club fees

Three money streams keep a grassroots team running: club subscriptions from families, local sponsors, and grants. Most teams rely on all three, in different proportions depending on the club.

What grassroots football sponsors actually pay for

The local sponsor on your shirt, usually a plumber, estate agent or the pub, is covering a specific kit bill, not funding the season. A typical deal is a few hundred pounds for the club's name on shirts and a thank-you post on social media. Some sponsors stretch to training tops or a share of tournament entry fees, but plan on the subscription covering everything else. Ask early: sponsors budget in the summer, before the kit order, not in October.

Grants and facility funding

Grants exist for bigger, one-off costs: goalpost replacement, pitch drainage, new changing rooms, coach education. The Football Foundation is the main route for facilities in England, and county FAs can point you towards smaller schemes. Grants rarely cover running costs like pitch hire or referee fees, so subscriptions carry the week-to-week bills. Your club secretary usually handles applications, but a volunteer who can draft the bid text is genuinely useful.

How do grassroots football kits get chosen, sourced and paid for?

A kit starts with a decision at club level: colours, crest and a supplier. Most clubs pick a catalogue brand, order through a local shop or an online kit printer, and pay from a mix of player subs and one sponsor's logo on the shirt. Because every affiliated club sits under a County FA, the crest and colours you register there are the ones that stick, so check them before you print anything.

Budget-wise, expect a one-off outlay every two to three seasons as kids grow out of shirts, plus mid-season extras like a keeper top. Many clubs stagger purchases: full kit one year, training tops the next, funded by a single local sponsor rather than ten small ones.

What are grassroots football tournaments and when do they happen?

Summer tournaments and festivals run mostly between May and July, once league seasons have finished, though some indoor and small-sided events appear through winter. Host clubs open their grounds on a Sunday, invite teams across age groups from Under 7s upwards, and run short round-robin groups into knockout rounds, with matches lasting 8–15 minutes each. Your squad plays four to six games in a morning, so every player usually gets meaningful minutes, which is why coaches use them for fair playing time rather than a first-choice XI.

Entry typically costs £25–£50 per team, paid to the host club, and places sell out fast for popular events, so book in spring. Practically, you will be juggling squad lists, car shares and meeting points in the group chat again, since most tournaments communicate by email and WhatsApp rather than a fixture system. Check with your league or county FA for approved events, and arrive early: parking and registration queues eat into your warm-up.

Who runs the matchday: volunteers, welfare officers and parents

On a Saturday, the unpaid work is split between a handful of people. The coach picks the line-up and runs the warm-up. The referee is often a parent with a whistle, or a paid official the home team sorts out. One parent collects match fees, another washes the kit, and someone texts the group chat with the pitch postcode and kick-off time.

The welfare officer is the one required role most clubs name on paper: they hold safeguarding records, DBS checks and first-aid certificates, and they are the escalation point if anything goes wrong. Funding for pitches and changing rooms behind all this comes largely from the Football Foundation, which distributes grants into grassroots facilities (Football Foundation).

The admin nobody warns you about

The real job is Sunday night: confirming availability, typing the line-up, chasing subs. Most of it lives in the group chat, which means it disappears the moment the season does.

How does a team website fit into grassroots football?

A team site sits alongside the group chat, not instead of the club. The FA, your league and your club handle registration, fixtures and discipline; the site is yours, for one team. What it should replace is the Sunday-night retyping: line-ups, kick-off times, player of the match, and the season's record that WhatsApp loses the moment someone's phone dies.

What a team site should and shouldn't do (including where ours falls short)

FAQ

Is grassroots football only for kids, or can adults play too?

Adults play it too. It covers open-age teams as well as youth football, including Sunday league sides for grown players. The FA counts it as the largest part of the game in England, and the definition stretches from Under 6s to adult teams run by volunteers. If nobody is paid to play, it qualifies.

Do you need FA qualifications to coach a grassroots youth team?

Most parent-coaches start with no formal qualification, just a DBS check and the club's welfare officer keeping records. County FAs and the FA run entry-level coaching courses, and clubs often pay for them once you've committed to a season. Check with your club's committee, since safeguarding requirements sit with them rather than with you.

How much does it cost to play for a grassroots football club per season?

Families pay club subscriptions, which cover pitch hire, referee fees and kit alongside the club's other bills. Local sponsors usually cover only the shirt, a few hundred pounds at most, and grants go towards one-off costs like goalposts or drainage, not running costs. Exact subs vary by club, so ask your treasurer.

Can a grassroots team get promoted into the professional leagues?

Not directly from a village club to the Premier League, but the pyramid connects. Semi-pro clubs at Step 1 to Step 5 of the non-league system pay players part-wages, so they sit above grassroots. A strong youth player can be scouted into an academy, which is the more common route towards professional football.

What's the difference between a grassroots club and a Sunday league team?

A club is the organisation: committee, bank account, affiliation to a county FA, often several teams across age groups. A Sunday league team is one adult side playing in a Sunday league, usually the club's open-age offering. Many grassroots clubs contain both youth teams and a Sunday league side under the same committee and crest.
