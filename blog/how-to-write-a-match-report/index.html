This guide gives you a kickoff-to-final-whistle structure, fill-in-the-blank templates, and grassroots examples, so your Sunday write-up takes minutes and actually gets read in the group chat.

What is a match report and what must it include?

A match report is a short written record of one game: what happened, who did it, and what it felt like from the touchline. For a grassroots team, it has one job above all others: letting a parent who missed the kick-off picture the morning without asking three people in the group chat.

The five things every football match report needs

  1. The score and the opposition — Under 9s won 3-2 against whom, at home or away.
  2. The scorers — names, and which goal was whose first of the season.
  3. Key moments — a save, a goal-line clearance, the moment the wind changed the game.
  4. Something about effort, not just outcome — the player who covered every blade of grass.
  5. A photo or two, if you have parental permission. The FA has published guidance on photographing and naming children in youth football publications, so check it before you tag names to faces.

What you can safely leave out

Leave out formations, possession stats and tactical analysis. An Under 10s match report is not a Liverpool match report. Also leave out anything you would not say to a child's face: a match report lives on the club site for the whole season, and kids re-read it. How do you structure a soccer match report from kickoff to final whistle?

Every match report needs seven parts in this order: a headline with the scoreline, a two-sentence summary, first half, second half, standout players, and a closing line. Once you've written one this way, the next one takes ten minutes.

The headline and first two sentences

Lead with the result: "Under 9s Lions 3–2 Rovers: first goal of the season for Sam." Then two sentences of summary covering who won, how, and the moment people will talk about. Parents scrolling the group chat decide in those two sentences whether to read on.

Body of the report, half by half

Split the body into first half and second half. Two or three sentences per half: key goals, one turning point, and one thing the team did well. Skip tactical detail; nobody at grassroots level wants a paragraph on shape.

The closing line that makes kids feel picked

Name your player of the match and one or two others who earned it, then finish with a forward-looking line about next week's fixture. If you keep every report on a team site like Touchline Tales, those named moments build into a season record each child can look back on.

What makes a good match report example versus a boring one?

Boring version, annotated

"Under 10s played a match today. It was a good game and both teams tried hard. Tommy scored. Final score 3-2."

What's wrong: no opponent named, no goalscorers beyond one, no moment anyone can picture, and "both teams tried hard" could describe any of 30 games this season.

Readable version, annotated

"Under 10s 3-2 Rovers Panthers, Saturday. 1-0 down at half time after a scrappy corner, then Ollie buried the equaliser five minutes into the second half. Maya won it with two minutes left, her first goal of the season, and the whole touchline knew about it. Player of the match: Sam, for two saves in the last five minutes. Everyone got at least half a game."

Why the second version works

The readable version wins because it trades summary for specifics: a scoreline with an opponent, a turning point, one player's milestone, and a nod to fair playing time. Those specifics are also what parents search for when they scroll the group chat on Sunday night. Keep it to five or six sentences, name every goalscorer, and give the player of the match one concrete reason, not "played really well." If you publish names and photos, check your club's policy first; The FA's safeguarding guidance sets the baseline for youth football, and your welfare officer checklist covers the photo-consent side. How should a grassroots club's match report differ from a Liverpool or Man U match report?

A pro match report is written for neutral readers who want analysis: xG, pressing traps, what the result means for the title race. A grassroots match report is written for about twenty families who already know the score. Same structure, opposite audience.

What pro coverage optimises for

A Liverpool or Man U match report leads with the decisive moment, quotes the manager, and dissects tactical decisions. It names two or three standout players and treats the rest as background. That works because the reader supports the club but doesn't know the squad personally.

What a grassroots match report football audience wants instead

Your Under 9s parents want to find their own child's name. So invert the pro formula:

Pro reportGrassroots report
Leads with the decisive goalLeads with a warm, one-line summary
Names 2–3 standout playersMentions every player who took part
Tactical analysisOne honest sentence on what the team worked on
Manager quotesA nod to the whole squad's effort

The rule: every child who turned up appears at least once, even in a heavy defeat. Fair playing time on the page matters as much as on the pitch.

How do you write a match report when you only saw part of the game?

Running the second pitch means missing half your own game. Write the report anyway: a shorter, honest one beats a gap in the season's record. Professional desks have the same rule: The Guardian's style guide says match reports should state plainly what the writer saw and did not, rather than pad around it (The Guardian style guide).

The three-questions shortcut

Ask three players, separately, before they hit the group chat: what was the best moment, who worked hardest, and what nearly went wrong. Their answers give you the middle of the report. You supply the score, the scorers, and one line on the weather from whoever was there.

The partial-view template

Use this when you saw only a stretch of the game:

Under 9s vs Riverside, 3-2 (won from 2-1 down) What I saw: [the 20 minutes you watched, one or two sentences]. What I missed: [the spell you were away, in plain words]. Player of the match: [name], for [reason from the players]. Next week: [one fixture detail].

Parents care that someone bothered; nobody expects a touchline volunteer to have seen every kick.

How do you write a match report quickly after a game?

Ten minutes on Sunday night is enough if you captured five notes at full time and fill in a template instead of writing from scratch. The trick is doing the thinking on the touchline, not at your laptop.

Five notes at full time

Before you leave the car park, type five things into your phone: the score and opponent, who scored and when, your player of the match and why, one moment worth retelling (a first goal, a save, a last-minute tackle), and anything you told the team afterwards. That is the whole report's raw material.

The fill-in-the-blank template

Keep a template with fixed slots: score, scorers, player of the match, key moment, next fixture. Paste the five notes into the slots, add two sentences of colour, done. The structure sections earlier in this guide give you the order; the template just stops you rewriting it every week.

Keeping line-ups and appearances without retyping

Line-ups are the slowest part, so stop retyping them. Keep last week's squad list and edit it down, or let a tool carry it over. How do you share a match report so parents and players actually read it?

Why WhatsApp kills match reports

The group chat is the worst place for a match report. A Sunday write-up sits below fifty replies about parking, lost shin pads and "who has the kit bag", and by Tuesday it is three hundred messages deep. Parents who missed the game never scroll back to find it, and by mid-season nobody can find the report from the cup quarter-final. Tools like Spond, which handles post-match communication alongside scheduling, still push updates through a feed that scrolls away. A feed is for now; a report deserves an address.

A team page as the season's archive

Post each match report on a team page instead, one entry per fixture, newest first. Parents get a link in the group chat that opens the report directly, and players can show grandparents their first goal write-up in April. By season's end you have a findable record: every line-up, every player of the match, every result. The honest caveat: a team page means one more place to post after the final whistle, so it only works if the report lives there first and WhatsApp gets the link, not the full text.

Which tools take the admin out of match reports?

Comparing the options honestly

Run every option through the same four tests: can you publish a match report in under ten minutes on a phone, does it keep a season-long archive, does it replace the group chat, and does it carry your club's crest and colours. Spond and Pitchero both handle availability and messaging well; check each against your club's setup before committing. FAQ

How long should a grassroots football match report be?

Five or six sentences. That covers a headline with the scoreline, two sentences of summary, a line or two per half, and a closing line naming your player of the match and next week's fixture. Parents scrolling the group chat on Sunday night decide in seconds, so short and specific beats long and vague every week.

Can I write a match report without knowing the tactics?

Yes, and you should skip tactics anyway. Grassroots parents want names and moments, not analysis of shape or pressing. Write what you saw: who scored, one turning point, one thing the team did well. If you only caught part of the game, say so plainly and ask three players for the best moment, the hardest worker, and what nearly went wrong.

What do you write in a match report for Under 7s where nobody keeps score?

Drop the scoreline and lead with the moments instead. Pick one or two highlights, like a first goal or a brave save, and mention every child who turned up at least once. Finish with a forward-looking line about next week. At that age the report is a keepsake for families, not a record of results.

Who should be named player of the match in a youth match report?

Name one child with a concrete reason, not "played really well": two saves in the last five minutes, or a first goal of the season. Rotate it across the season so different kids earn the mention. You can add one or two others who stood out, but keep the headline award to a single named player each week.

Do I need permission to name children in a match report?

Check your club's policy first. The FA's safeguarding guidance sets the baseline for youth football, and your welfare officer handles photo consent separately. Many clubs require parental permission before publishing names or photos anywhere public, including a team page. Ask before the season starts so writing Sunday's report never turns into a safeguarding question.
