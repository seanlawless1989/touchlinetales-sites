Fair playing time means every player gets a minimum number of minutes you decide before the season starts, communicated to parents in advance and recorded match by match. This guide gives you copy-and-use rotation templates, a minimum-guarantee rule, word-for-word scripts for awkward parent conversations, and a low-effort way to keep a season-long record without spreadsheets.

What does fair playing time actually mean in youth and grassroots sport?

Fair playing time is not one rule. It is one of three ideas, and knowing which one you are promising your squad saves arguments later.

Equal minutes, meaningful minutes, minimum guarantee: three definitions

Equal minutes means every player gets the same time on the pitch each match. Meaningful minutes means everyone plays, but not identically: a keeper gets a full half, a new Under 8 gets short runs in a position they can succeed in. A minimum guarantee sits between the two: you commit in writing that nobody plays less than, say, half the game, and you keep a season-long record so you can prove it. The FA's guidance on youth football formats and respect standards underpins the equal-participation expectation for young age groups (The FA).

How the answer changes from Under 7s to Under 11s and beyond

At Under 7s, equal minutes is the only defensible answer: development is the point, results are not. From Under 9s to Under 11s, a minimum guarantee with rotating positions works better as children specialise. At older ages, meaningful minutes become fair, provided the policy is written down, shared with parents, and tracked. Whatever you promise, put it somewhere parents can check, not buried in the group chat; our data processing terms at Touchline Tales cover how we handle any player records you keep with us.

How much playing time should every player get per match?

A safe benchmark: at Under 7 to Under 11 level, aim for every player to get at least 50% of available minutes, and treat anything below a third as a decision you need to justify to parents. In a 40-minute 7-a-side match with 12 players, total player-minutes are 280, which is 23 minutes per child if shared equally. As squads shrink or matches get longer, that number rises, so work it out per match rather than guessing.

The minimum-guarantee rule and how to state it

State one number to parents at the start of the season: "Every player gets at least half the match, every week." Then honour it even when a game is tight, because the promise only counts if it survives a 3-2 scoreline.

How do you create a rotation policy that parents understand?

Write the policy before the season starts, not after the first complaint. One page, plain English, and shared with every parent at the pre-season meeting so nobody hears it for the first time on a touchline in October.

The one-page policy: what to include

Keep it to five things: your minimum guarantee (for example, every player starts at least half the matches), how you rotate positions, how you handle players arriving late or missing training, how long a player sits before coming back on, and one line on what happens when a match is tight. Say explicitly that equal playing time and fair playing time are not the same thing, and what fair means for your age group. For younger teams like Under 7s to Under 11s, lean towards near-equal minutes; for older teams, be honest that minutes will vary.

Sharing it where parents actually look

Do not bury it in the group chat between fixture changes and lift requests. Send it as a single pinned message in WhatsApp, ask parents to confirm they have read it, and keep the original somewhere findable all season. If a parent later queries minutes, you are pointing at a document they agreed to, not arguing from memory. If a conversation does get heated, the FA's published safeguarding guidance on managing parent behaviour is a useful reference for keeping things calm and child-focused.

What should you do when a player complains about their playing time?

Listen first, promise nothing on the touchline, and follow up with a short private chat within a couple of days. A complaint handled calmly is a sign the player trusts you enough to raise it.

In the moment: what to say on the touchline

Keep it brief and neutral. Try: "I hear you. Not this week, but you'll get your minutes. Come and see me after training and we'll talk properly." That acknowledges the feeling without rewriting your plan mid-match or making a promise you can't keep. Avoid justifying the decision in front of the squad or other parents; it turns one player's question into a public debate. If a parent raises it instead, the same line works: "Happy to talk after full time, not during."

After the match: the follow-up conversation

Within a day or two, have a two-minute chat away from the group. Be specific about what the player needs to work on, and specific about when minutes will come: "You'll start next week's friendly." Vague encouragement ("your chance will come") is what breeds repeat complaints. If your rotation policy guarantees minimum minutes, show the player how it works so the answer feels like a plan, not a favour. Finish with one concrete thing to improve before the next match, and check they left the conversation still wanting to play.

How do you communicate playing time decisions to parents without conflict?

Most conflict after full time comes from surprise, not disagreement. A parent who has seen your rotation policy in writing before the season, and sees the minutes recorded each week, rarely texts angry. Say the policy out loud at the pre-season meeting, repeat it in the group chat, and keep a running record so your decisions match your promises. Youth sport bodies such as the Youth Sport Trust publish guidance for adults supporting children in community sport, and the consistent message is that clear, regular communication from the coach keeps parents on side. The scripts below work because they assume good intent, name the policy rather than your mood, and never argue in text.

Script: the parent who messages after full time

"Thanks for the message. I rotate everyone to keep playing time fair across the season, and I track minutes each week so I can show you the picture over the whole year, not just one match. Happy to talk it through on a call if you'd like."

Script: the parent who wants their child playing striker

"I hear you. I rotate positions so every player tries different roles this season, including striker. If they keep working in training, they'll get their chances there."

When to move from the group chat to a phone call

One exchange of texts is fine. If a second message arrives, or the topic is their child's confidence or a comparison with another player, call instead. Text strips out tone, and a written thread you both can reread only raises the stakes. A ten-minute call ends most of these conversations; the same one in writing rarely ends well.

When is it OK to give star players more minutes?

Legitimate reasons minutes will not be equal

Uneven minutes are defensible when they rest on something the whole squad can see, not on who scored last week. Attendance is the clearest one: a player who turns up to training gets priority over one who does not, and most parents accept that without argument. Attitude counts too, within reason, but it should never become a code for "my favourite".

Cup matches and semi-finals tempt everyone to load the strongest XI, and that is fine if your policy says so in advance. The RFU's age-grade guidance encourages rotating game time across the season rather than within every single match, so a stronger player can bank extra minutes one week and a developing player the next (England Rugby).

Being upfront instead of pretending

The mistake is not unequal minutes, it is pretending they are equal. If you plan to play your strongest side in the cup, tell parents before the match, not after. State your criteria once, apply them to every player including your own child, and nobody can accuse you of hiding anything.

How can you track playing time across a season without spreadsheets?

The options: notebook, spreadsheet, or a team site

A paper notebook at pitchside is the lowest-friction option, but it lives in one bag and dies the moment it gets soaked at Under 9s in November. A spreadsheet fixes the storage problem but creates a new one: somebody has to retype minutes every Sunday night, and the volunteer coach doing line-ups already has enough Sunday nights. A team site automates part of that, though most tools built for clubs, like Pitchero, are aimed at whole-club needs rather than one team's season record.

FAQ

Do FA rules say every child must play in a grassroots match?

No. The FA does not have a rule requiring a set number of minutes per child, though its youth football guidance underpins equal participation for young age groups. Some leagues set their own minimums, so check your competition rules. In practice, the promise that matters is the one you write down and share with parents before the season starts.

Should weaker players get the same minutes as the best player at Under 10?

At Under 10, aim for near-equal minutes with a written minimum guarantee, such as every player getting at least half the match. Development, not results, is the point at this age. Rotate positions so weaker players get short runs where they can succeed, and track minutes across the season so you can show parents the record rather than argue from memory.

What do I do when a parent complains to the club rather than to me?

Ask the club official to point the parent back to your written rotation policy, which they agreed to at the start of the season. Then request a phone call rather than a written thread, since text strips out tone. Stay calm and child-focused; the FA's safeguarding guidance on managing parent behaviour is a useful reference if the conversation escalates.

How do you rotate a squad of 15 in a 9-a-side match?

In a 60-minute match split into four 15-minute quarters, there are 540 player-minutes to share, which averages 36 per child. Rotate six players off each quarter: nine players finish on 45 minutes and six on 30. Write the quarter-by-quarter line-ups before kick-off so you swap on the whistle instead of doing arithmetic mid-match.
